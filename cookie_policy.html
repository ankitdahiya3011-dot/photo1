Cookies are a core part of how the modern web works and bring many benefits, but they can also raise privacy questions. This extended cookie policy gives a full overview of the cookie practices used on our website at energylife.net

What are Cookies?

Cookies are small text files holding limited amounts of information that are downloaded and stored on your computer or mobile device when you visit a website. On each subsequent visit, cookies are sent back to the website that created them, or to another website that recognizes them. Cookies let websites recognize a user's device and remember certain details about your visit, your preferences, and your activity online.
Cookies perform many different tasks that make browsing simpler and more personalized. For instance, cookies remember your preferences on sites you visit frequently, help tailor content and recommendations to your interests, help determine whether traffic to a site comes from a real person or a bot, and supply anonymized data about how visitors interact with a website.
The main categories of cookies:
  • First Party Cookies — cookies set by the domain of the website you are visiting. Only that domain can read them.
  • Third Party Cookies — cookies set by domains other than the one you are visiting. For example, a website may partner with external advertisers, analytics companies, or social media platforms to deliver relevant content, ads, and plugins.
  • Session Cookies — cookies that exist only for the duration of your online session and are removed from your device once you close your browser. They allow a website to link the actions a visitor takes within a single session.
  • Persistent Cookies — cookies that remain on your device between browsing sessions until they expire or are deleted. They make it possible to store preferences or actions across multiple visits and sites.
    • How long a cookie remains on your device depends on whether it is a session or persistent cookie and on its expiration time.

    How to Manage Cookie Preferences?

    You can control cookie preferences on websites through your browser settings and other tools:
  • Browser Cookie Settings
    • Your browser settings let you enable, disable, and delete cookies for some or all websites. Bear in mind that blocking cookies entirely may degrade your browsing experience, since many websites depend on cookies to work correctly. Every browser handles cookie management a little differently, so consult the help section of your preferred browser: Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge, Explorer.
  • Cookie Consent Management Platform
    • Our website uses a consent management platform that lets users opt in or out of specific cookie categories. The platform shows a banner at the top or bottom of the site that explains the types of cookies in use and lets you tailor your preferences.
  • Blocking and Deleting Cookies
    • Beyond browser settings, there are utilities that can block and remove cookies:
    1. Ghostery lets you block and delete cookies and see who is setting them.
    2. CCleaner removes cookies and optimizes browsers.

  • Do Not Track
    • Some browsers offer Do Not Track features that let you signal your preference not to be tracked by websites. At present, no global standard has been established for how websites should respond to Do Not Track signals. Consequently, this website is unlikely to intercept and respond to such signals. Other tracking prevention measures, such as incognito browsing mode, can help instead.
    Other Tracking Technologies
    Alongside traditional browser cookies, other tracking technologies exist:

  • Web Beacons
    • These tiny transparent graphic images (also called pixel tags or clear gifs) can be used to track users' movements online. For example, web beacons can count page views, deliver cookies, detect Adobe Flash versions, and more.

  • ETags
    • An ETag, or entity tag, is an opaque identifier used in web caching and conditional HTTP requests. ETags allow websites to check whether a locally stored version of a file matches the version on the web server. They make loading cached resources more efficient, but carry implications for user privacy and tracking.
  • Local Storage Objects (LSOs)
    • LSOs, such as Flash cookies, are stored as data files on your device and can be used to remember settings, usage, and other site-specific data.
  • Browser Fingerprinting
    • This technique combines details about your device — such as software versions and configurations — to build a unique fingerprint that can then be used for tracking without cookies.
  • Deep Packet Inspection
    • Deep Packet Inspection examines both the header and the payload of internet traffic, allowing very detailed monitoring and tracking of internet users and their activity.
  • History Sniffing
    • Some websites covertly perform cross-site history sniffing by styling visited links differently from unvisited ones. This lets them infer your browsing history and build profiles of your interests and behavior.
  • Canvas Fingerprinting
    • The HTML canvas element can extract data about your device and computer settings, such as font preferences. That information can be used to fingerprint browsers.
  • Device Recognition
    • Cross-device tracking techniques, such as deterministic matching, attempt to identify and link devices to one another, enabling persistent tracking across desktop, mobile, and tablet.
  • Location Tracking
    • Geolocation features in browsers and devices can let websites collect your location data. Location can be inferred from your IP address, Wi-Fi networks, or GPS.
